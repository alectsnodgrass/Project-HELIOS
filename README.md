# Executive Summary

### Elevator Pitch

Project HELIOS transformed an incomplete, nonoperational golf cart into a working platform for evaluating solar-assisted electric transportation. In partnership with LANI-EV, a division of LANI-Solar, the team restored Tennessee Tech’s Club Car, integrated a lithium iron phosphate battery and solar-charging system, and evaluated how solar energy replenishes the battery relative to vehicle energy use. The project also produced an adaptable solar-panel mounting kit and a simulated four-servo control concept that directs the panel toward the sun while the cart is parked. Together, these outcomes provide practical designs, performance evidence, and repeatable documentation to support future solar-powered vehicle retrofits.

### Abstract

Solar-assisted low-speed vehicles offer an opportunity to supplement grid charging with onboard renewable energy. Their practical value depends on reliable integration, adaptable mounting hardware, and an understanding of how solar generation compares with vehicle energy demand. Working with LANI-EV, Tennessee Technological University’s Electrical and Computer Engineering Project HELIOS addressed these challenges through vehicle restoration, retrofit implementation, performance testing, and control-system design.

The team restored an inherited Club Car that arrived partially disassembled and without batteries or a steering wheel. A lithium iron phosphate battery and solar-charging system were integrated, supported by an illustrated conversion procedure, as-built electrical documentation, and installation records. Post-conversion testing characterized solar charging and vehicle discharge under documented operating conditions, providing an evidence-based assessment of the system’s energy balance.

To improve installation adaptability, the team surveyed roof configurations across three major vehicle brands and developed a functional mounting-kit prototype designed to accommodate selected models from at least two brands. The team also developed and evaluated a concept for parked-vehicle solar tracking. Using vehicle-state, solar optics, and position tracking the algorithm generated coordinated commands for four servos within a modeled range of panel motion. Simulation evaluated orientation calculations, operating-state transitions, and responses to invalid inputs.

The completed project delivered a restored demonstration vehicle, retrofit documentation, performance data, a mounting-kit engineering package, and a simulated solar-tracking concept. These deliverables establish a foundation for LANI-EV and future engineering teams to refine solar-assisted vehicle designs and assess their suitability for specific operating conditions.


# Capabilities

The completed project combines a solar-assisted vehicle retrofit, adaptable mounting hardware, and a maximum power point solar-tracking concept.

 - **Operational vehicle platform:** A restored Tennessee Tech Club Car supports demonstration and testing of the LANI-EV solar retrofit.

 - **Solar-assisted battery charging:** A lithium iron phosphate (LiFePO₄) battery and compatible solar-charging system supply vehicle power and replenish stored energy when sunlight is available.

 - **Performance characterization:** Recorded charging and discharge data show how solar input compares with vehicle energy demand under documented operating conditions.

 - **Adaptable panel mounting:** A functional mounting-kit prototype provides a common attachment approach designed for selected models from at least two major vehicle brands. Engineering drawings and installation instructions define its supported configurations.

 - **Simulated solar tracking:** A control algorithm uses vehicle-state, compass-heading, date/time, and geographic-location inputs to calculate panel orientation and generate coordinated commands for four servos. Simulation covers parked-operation logic, motion limits, and invalid-input handling.

 - **Repeatable engineering documentation:** Conversion instructions, wiring diagrams, component records, test procedures, and editable design files support maintenance and future development.

Full-scale powered panel positioning remains a future implementation step. Solar charging supplements the battery’s energy supply; grid-free operation depends on sunlight, driving conditions, and vehicle usage.


# Salient Outcomes

 - Restoration became a substantial engineering contribution. The inherited vehicle arrived disassembled, without batteries or a steering wheel, and with extra, unnecessary components. Establishing a functional test platform required evaluating and restoring the vehicle before completing the retrofit.

 - The project connected solar generation to actual vehicle demand. Charge-versus-discharge testing provided a practical basis for evaluating solar assistance under specific operating conditions.

 - The mounting design addressed more than one vehicle configuration. A three-brand roof survey informed an adaptable kit architecture, with shared components and model-specific interfaces documented for future refinement.

 - Parked time became an opportunity for further design exploration. The tracking concept explored how panel orientation could follow the sun while the vehicle is stationary. 

 - The documentation supports continued development. The project leaves future teams with a traceable record of the vehicle configuration, installation process, test results, and design assumptions.

## Stakeholders
The primary customer for this project is Lani Electric Vehicles (LaniEV), a subsidiary of Lani Solar, LLC. Lani Solar develops solar-integrated mobility platforms and energy solutions intended to reduce grid dependence and lifecycle operating costs in light electric vehicle applications. LaniEV is developing standardized retrofit conversion kits for low-speed electric vehicles (LSVs), personal transportation vehicles (PTVs), and golf cars that replace legacy lead-acid battery systems with LiFePO₄ battery banks integrated with solar charging capability. The company’s objective is to enable scalable fleet conversions by providing a structured and repeatable retrofit platform supported by validated engineering documentation.

Secondary stakeholders include organizations that utilize LSV fleets, such as golf resorts, hotels, universities, airports, and industrial plants. Organizations such as these rely on LSVs for day-to-day logistics and are directly affected by reliability, lifecycle cost, and maintenance requirements. Formal documentation of the retrofit process and post-conversion performance data provides decision-making support for these stakeholders by enabling informed evaluation of economic and operational impact. Secondary stakeholders also include Tennessee Technological University (TTU), as the owner of the control golf car used for the project, and as the ensuring agency for documented safety, reliability, and performance data. Dr. Christopher Storm Johnson is responsible, as the capstone instructor, for evaluating team performance and ensuring the project's desired end-state is reached.
